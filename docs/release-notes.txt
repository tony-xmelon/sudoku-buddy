PENCILLED NOTES ARE NO LONGER READ AS ANSWERS

Photograph a puzzle booklet part way through and you photograph two kinds of
pencil: the answers, and the little lists of candidates squeezed into the squares
you have not settled yet. The app read some of the lists as answers, and a wrong
answer in a square is worse than no answer at all - it is a digit you did not
write, in a puzzle you now have to correct by hand.

It had a reason. A candidate mark is normally faint and normally sits along the
top of its square, and either of those gives it away. But a list written firmly
into a cramped square is neither: it is pressed nearly as hard as an answer, it
goes wherever there is room, and where one figure touches its neighbour the pair
measures two thirds the height of a printed digit, which is tall enough to pass
for something written.

Nothing about such a mark on its own says what it is. What says so is the rest of
the page. Where your handwriting is plainly bigger than the print - which is what
a booklet gets, a small printed face in a large square - that hand has no small
figures in it, and something at two thirds the height of the print did not come
from it. So the page is now asked where its own handwriting lies, and anything far
below that is put back to being a note.

The same measure was going wrong in the other direction and is fixed with it. A
printed clue with a stray pencil stroke fused to it, or a clue somebody has drawn
a ring round, measures too tall to be print and was offered as an answer. Its size
really is wrong and no size rule can save it; what is still right about it is the
ink, because a press lays down more than a pencil can and a stray stroke is not
heavy enough to dilute that. Those clues are given back to the press.

Four photographs of one booklet page, taken over three hours as it was solved,
found all of this. Every printed clue on all four is read, every answer on all
four is read, and one square out of three hundred and twenty-four is still sorted
wrongly - a note written so large and so firmly, over the grey of two rubbed-out
marks, that only its colour would tell you.
