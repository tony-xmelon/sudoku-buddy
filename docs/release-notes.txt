Two fixes for the camera, on a screen where the keypad sits under the puzzle.

THE OUTLINE NO LONGER SWALLOWS THE NUMBER PAD

A sudoku app on a screen prints its keypad directly under the grid - three rows of
nine, ruled in the same face at the same pitch. The rectangle round the puzzle and
the pad together is regular enough to look like a grid and half as big again as
the puzzle, and the app preferred it on size.

That preference was put in for a newspaper, which prints last week's solution
under this week's puzzle and prints it more neatly, so the better-looking grid is
the wrong one and size is what tells them apart. It had one half of the test and
not the other: a candidate had to be clearly a grid before its size counted, but
nothing asked whether it was the right shape. A sudoku is square. The pad and the
puzzle together are a third taller than wide.

Size now only wins among candidates as square as the best grid on the page. The
comparison is against the page's own best rather than a fixed bar, because a page
photographed at an angle is genuinely oblong in the frame - but everything in one
photograph is foreshortened alike, so a shape that is still oblong after that is
oblong in life.

A BLINK NO LONGER RESETS THE SHUTTER

Automatic capture waits for a run of frames in which the grid is found, held
still, and framed properly. Any single frame that found nothing threw the whole
run away - and it does not take a moving camera to produce one of those, only a
breath, a reflection, or the autofocus hunting for a moment. On a page where the
reader was right nine frames in ten the shutter never fired at all: you held
still, watched the outline blink, and pressed the button yourself.

Two blank frames in a row are now forgiven. Nothing else is relaxed - the frames
that count still have to be a grid the app accepts, with no complaint and no
movement - and a camera that has really left the page misses far more than two in
a row and starts again as before.
